The model was fine.
The dataset was lying.

A data-understanding study built from a real forecasting problem: can disruptions in global logistics predict U.S. import-price changes before they appear in official price data?
Research rule: hold the downstream model accountable to the data. Predeclare the test, preserve provenance, and invalidate analyses when the data semantics fail.
Research context

I combine U.S. Census Port–HS trade records, BLS import-price indexes, the New York Fed Global Supply Chain Pressure Index, and AIS-derived maritime activity. Products are represented through historical country–port exposure networks, and forecasts are evaluated 1–6 months ahead using rolling, availability-aware splits.

The original economics hypothesis repeatedly failed out of sample. That failure became the more general question explored here: which choices in dataset construction change what a fixed downstream model learns, and which apparent gains disappear under stricter data semantics?

03 / FALSIFICATION

Four increasingly specific hypotheses. None cleared the gate.

The practical success criterion was frozen at ≥5% lower 3-month RMSE: RRMSE ≤ 0.95. Results above 1.0 are worse than the baseline.

Negative results changed the product decision

Aggregate pressure, granular graph structure, route topology, and AIS throughput all failed to produce a stable forecasting edge. That prevented months of product work on a signal that did not survive out-of-sample evaluation.

Quantize the data distribution, then test what coverage buys you.

what changed?

Coverage improved. Signal did not.

At 25% of the training-data budget, VQ-balanced selection preserves 100% of the 32 learned states versus 89.1% under uniform random selection.

Its mean future-holdout RMSE is 2.6531 versus 2.6597 for random sampling and 2.6533 for the full-data model. Better coverage does not create predictive signal that is not present in the data.

05 / SHIFT AUDIT

Does the codebook describe the future—or only the training distribution?

Coverage is not enough. The frozen 32-code representation is used as a diagnostic instrument: assign the strictly later 2025–2026 holdout to the training codebook, measure occupancy shift, then ask whether state support relates to downstream error.

The representation exposes temporal redistribution.

The training distribution uses all 32 codes with an effective-state count of 25.1. The future holdout activates only 17 codes and has an effective-state count of 8.2. The train/holdout occupancy distributions differ by 0.360 bits of Jensen–Shannon divergence.

downstream behavior

Low-support states are where the model is least reliable.

Across the 17 codes that appear in the future holdout, historical state support and state-conditioned holdout RMSE have an exploratory Spearman association of ρ = -0.50: rarer training states tend to carry larger future error.

That makes the VQ useful as a data-understanding instrument, even though it does not create a forecasting edge by itself.

Practical significance check. At the 25% training-data budget, VQ-balanced sampling lowers mean RMSE by only 0.0066 versus random sampling (about 0.24%), and only 59% of paired seeds favor VQ. The paired bootstrap interval for the mean difference is [-0.0098, -0.0036]. Statistically detectable is not the same as practically useful.
06 / TAKEAWAY
The most important model improvement was learning when not to trust the dataset.

Data semantics first

Hierarchy, provenance, and availability can dominate algorithm choice. Structural invariants belong upstream of model metrics.

Representations need downstream tests

Quantization exposed coverage and redundancy, but coverage alone did not make a weak signal useful.

Falsification is product work

A failed stage gate is not wasted analysis when it prevents deployment of a brittle or non-incremental data signal.
